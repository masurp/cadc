MA Communication Science · Period 2 · 2026

How this course works

Digital communication leaves more text behind than anyone can read: political messaging, news, social posts, support logs, open survey answers. This course is about turning that text into numbers so a computer can analyse it, and about finding out whether those numbers measure what you think they do.

New here? Start with Before the first lab (about an hour). How you are graded is on Assessment, what you may use AI for on AI use, and every date and room on Weeks. All rules in one document: the course manual (PDF).

WHAT YOU WILL LEARN

01

Framing the problem

Spot the data-analytic problem inside a communication question, state it precisely enough that a machine could attempt it, and judge whether a computational method can answer it at all.

02

Understanding the pipeline

Describe the text-classification pipeline end to end (obtain, represent, classify, validate) and name the point where each decision can go wrong. In R: import text, wrangle it into a tidy table, turn it into tokens, counts and embeddings.

03

Knowing what each method is for

Word counts, dictionaries, classic machine learning, word embeddings, large language models: what each costs and assumes, and when the cheapest one is good enough. In R: apply each of them to the same texts.

04

Validating a measurement

Build a gold standard, compute coder agreement, report precision, recall and F1 honestly, read a confusion matrix, and decide when a number is not good enough to publish.

05

Code that others can check

Make an analysis reproducible from a clean start, document the decisions behind it, and disclose what a tool did and where your own judgement shaped the outcome.

06

A study of your own

Develop a research question, run a small text-analysis study in a group, and present your findings to the room.

HOW A WEEK WORKS

Eight weeks in three parts: four weeks of methods, the exam in week 5, and three weeks on a project of your own. In the four weeks of methods, every week has the same rhythm.

Monday · lecture

The problem, then the method

Each week opens with a research problem and works toward a method that could solve it. The arc runs from counting words in the 1960s to prompting a language model today. Lectures are not mandatory, but the exam is built on them.

Tuesday & Thursday · labs · mandatory

Working in R yourself

You work in RStudio on real data, in a group of about twenty. You predict what a chunk will output before you run it, find planted bugs, and on Thursday start the week’s homework with your teacher in the room. Each lab opens with a short check-in.

Sunday · homework

Finish what you started on Thursday

Your code, its output, three short reflections on your own results, and an AI disclosure. Four in total, one each Sunday of weeks 1 to 4.

How the homework works

WHAT WE EXPECT FROM YOU

  • Bring your own laptop to every lab, with R, RStudio and the course packages installed and the setup check passed.
  • Run everything yourself. Reading along teaches you less than it seems to, and the exam tests what you only learn by doing it.
  • Say something. Labs run on discussion: about a coding decision, a metric, a result that looks too good.
  • Say how you used AI. The rules are on the AI use page, and they are more permissive than you probably expect.
