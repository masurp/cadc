I · What changed since 2013

Three advances

  • Representation. From counts to word vectors to contextual vectors: a word’s numbers now depend on the sentence it is in
  • Pre-training and transfer. A model trained once, on an enormous corpus, for a general task — then reused for our task, with a little fine-tuning or none at all
  • Scale. The same architecture, thousands of times larger, trained on most of the written web; abilities nobody trained for appeared along the way

The three compound. The instrument we get is a model that reads a text and can be told, in words, what to do with it.

II · Inside a transformer

Attention is all you need

  • Until 2017 the models that read sequences did so one word at a time, carrying a memory forward — slow to train, and forgetful over long distances
  • Vaswani et al. (2017), at Google, dropped the recurrence: every word looks at every other word at once, through a mechanism they called attention
  • The consequences: training could be parallelised, so models could be made much larger; and distant words could influence each other directly
  • The paper was about translation. The architecture — the transformer — is the backbone of BERT, GPT, Llama, Claude, Gemini and everything else in this deck

One architecture, 2017, cited a hundred and fifty thousand times. Everything since is that block, stacked and scaled.

Vaswani, A., Shazeer, N., Parmar, N., Uszkoreit, J., Jones, L., Gomez, A. N., Kaiser, Ł., & Polosukhin, I. (2017). Attention is all you need. Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems, 30, 5998–6008.

How a word’s vector picks up its context

Click 2: last week’s static embeddings, one vector per word, and above each content word a picture of what that vector “sees” on its own: a dog, but no particular dog; blue, but nothing that is blue. The numbers are illustrative. Click 3: the position is added in — one to ten — without it the model would have a bag of words. Click 4: attention. Every word looks at every other, faintly; the strong lines are where the weight goes — angry and blue into dog, red and haired into man. Inside a transformer

Eight clicks; keep it to eight minutes. Tokens (1): the pieces the model has a number for. Embeddings (2): last week’s word vectors, looked up in a table, and Harry’s is the same vector whoever Harry is. Position (3): a second vector for where the word stands; without it, a bag of words. Attention (4) is the new idea: Harry asks every other token how relevant it is, and the weights are real (BERT, layer 4, head 0, measured: 96 percent on Potter). Update (5): Harry takes a weighted share of what the others carry, and its vector changes; on the right, the real curve: the two Harrys start identical and drift apart layer by layer.

Feed-forward (6): attention moved information between tokens. Now each vector goes through a network like week 3’s on its own, the same network for every token. On the right, Harry’s vector, which now carries Potter. Each hidden unit responds to a pattern in the vector; the lit one responds to Harry together with Potter. The network’s output is added to the vector, so Harry and Potter stay and wizard and Hogwarts join them. Much of what a model learned in pre-training is stored in these units (Geva et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2022). BERT has 3,072 of them in every block, Llama 3.1 8B has 14,336. The word labels are illustrative; real dimensions do not come with names.

Many layers (7): attention plus feed-forward is one block; BERT stacks twelve, the model in the cold open thirty-two. Why more than one: a block gathers once and looks up once, and every block works on what the blocks below added. In an early block Harry picks up Potter and then wizard. Only in a later block can the castle look at Harry, find a wizard there and become Hogwarts; with a single block the castle would only see the plain Harry. Roughly, lower blocks work out which pieces belong together, middle blocks add what the model knows, and upper blocks prepare what comes next (Tenney et al., 2019, for BERT). The three bands simplify a gradual change.

The next token (8): after the top block, the vector of the last token, the full stop, gets one score for each of the 128,256 tokens in Llama’s vocabulary. A softmax turns the scores into probabilities that sum to one. Week 3’s logistic regression did the same for three classes; here there are 128,256. After the full stop the model expects a new sentence (The 10%, But 8%, He 7%), and no token gets more than ten percent.

Two models on one drawing, on purpose: the attention and the curve are BERT’s, because it reads in both directions and its heads are legible; the bars are Llama’s, because it predicts the next token. Query, key and value are named in words; the matrices are in the reading.

Two ways to read a sentence

Encoder · reads in both directions

BERT · Google, 2018RoBERTa · MetaXLM-R · multilingualDeBERTa · MicrosoftModernBERT · 2024nomic-embed · week 3
  • Every word’s vector has read the whole text, including what follows it, so not sees good: what a classifier needs
  • 110 to 600 million weights and one pass per text, with nothing to write. Fine-tuned on a few thousand coded texts, it often beats a prompted LLM (Bucher & Martini, 2024)

Decoder · reads left to right

GPT · OpenAILlama · MetaGemma · GoogleQwen · AlibabaMistralClaude · Anthropic
  • Predicting the next token, again and again, is all that writing text takes. An answer, a translation and a label are all continuations of a prompt
  • Any task without training, because the task goes into the prompt. The price is billions of weights and a full pass for every token it writes

Bucher, M. J. J., & Martini, M. (2024). Fine-tuned “small” LLMs (still) significantly outperform zero-shot generative AI models in text classification. arXiv:2406.08660.

The same block, used two ways. Point at the arcs. In the encoder, not draws on every other word, on both sides, so its vector already contains good when the classifier on top reads it. In the decoder, not may only look back; the position after it is empty, because predicting it is the whole job. That one restriction is what makes a decoder a text generator: write a token, append it, predict the next.

Then the trade. For generation the decoder is enough, and for almost everything else, since any task can be written as a prompt and continued. For classification at scale an encoder is the efficient choice: hundreds of millions of weights instead of billions, one pass per text instead of one per generated token, and fine-tuned on our coded sample it often beats zero-shot prompting (Bucher & Martini, 2024, across several classification tasks). Week 3’s sentence embeddings came from an encoder. The table on the next slide sums up the differences.

From a base model to a chatbot

Five clicks, and the last two are the ones worth the time. Card 1 is pre-training: predict the next token, on trillions of tokens of web pages, books, code and transcripts. Nobody labels anything; the text is the label, every next word a right answer, hence “self-supervised”. To predict well the model has to represent grammar, facts, style, who Harry is and what not good means, as side effects of the objective. Everything a model knows about your construct it learned this way, from text nobody chose for your study. Months, tens of thousands of chips, paid for once by the vendor, and what comes out only continues text. Card 2: a second, tiny phase — tens of thousands of instruction-and-answer pairs, written by people — and now it answers the question you asked. Card 3: people are shown two answers and say which is better; a reward model learns to imitate that judgement, so the preference can be applied a million times without them.

Click 4 opens what “reinforcement learning” actually means, because the room hears the phrase daily and has usually never seen it: the model writes an answer, a score comes back, the weights move toward higher scores, and round again. That is the whole idea — no labelled right answer, just a signal that this was better than that.

Click 5 is the 2026 sentence: where the score comes from is the whole difference. From people, you get a chat model tuned to be liked. From an automatic check — does the code run, is the proof right — you get a reasoning model, and nobody has to watch. Say that the numbers on the cards are orders of magnitude, not measurements: the vendors do not publish this any more, as the size chart already showed.

What the tuning left in the model

  • The model you prompt is a chat model: its label on “Not good at all” is a next-token prediction shaped by all three phases
  • The answer format, the hedging, the refusal to pick a side on a mixed review — trained in, by somebody else, for a general user
  • Preference tuning optimises for what a rater liked, which is not the same as what your codebook calls correct; a fluent, agreeable answer scores well either way
  • A base model, before any of this, is worse at following your instruction and less shaped by anyone’s idea of a good answer. Neither is neutral

You are prompting an instrument that was tuned to be helpful to somebody else. A coder following your codebook is something else.

Transfer, and two ways to use it

  • Transfer learning is the ability: what a model learned in pre-training carries over to tasks it was never trained on. Nobody trained Llama to code film reviews, and it codes them
  • Fine-tuning uses it by training further on our coded sample, a few hundred to a few thousand texts, until the weights fit our codebook. The BERT route; it needs a GPU and week 3’s split and test
  • Prompting is simply how we talk to the model. The codebook goes into the input and the weights stay as they are. The route of this lecture
  • Fine-tuning still wins on narrow tasks with enough coded data (Alizadeh et al., 2025); prompting wins on cost of entry

Transfer is what the pre-trained model brings. Fine-tuning and prompting are two ways to use it, and this week uses the one without a training loop.

III · The models

What a large language model is

  • A transformer decoder with billions of weights, pre-trained to predict the next token on a large share of all written text, then tuned to answer
  • “Large” is doing the work: the same architecture at a hundred million weights writes plausible sentences; at a hundred billion it translates, summarises, codes and follows a codebook it has never seen
  • It runs as a service (an API you send text to) or as weights you download and run yourself
  • What it is not: a database, a search engine, a reasoner with a model of the world, or a coder who read your instructions carefully. It is a next-token machine that has read a great deal

Everything an LLM does for you is generated. Including the label, including the confidence, including the explanation.

schema <- tidyllm_schema(
  name       = "coding",
  label      = field_fct("the code", .levels = c("positive", "negative")),
  confidence = field_dbl("how sure you are, 0 to 1"),

Zero-shot, few-shot, chain of thought

Only the middle of the prompt changes: few-shot adds to what goes in, chain of thought to what comes back. Which of them helps is measured on the next slides, on forty-eight texts.

Three prompt designs. Zero-shot: the codebook and the text, and the model’s general ability does the work. Few-shot: a handful of coded examples in the prompt, which anchor the categories the way a coder-training session does. Chain of thought: the model is asked to reason before it answers; slower by an order of magnitude, sometimes more accurate on hard categories, and it leaves a rationale to read. Which one helps depends on the task, the model and the codebook, so it is measured, not assumed.

Read it left to right and the whole comparison is one idea: the codebook and the text are identical in all three columns, so anything that differs in the answer comes from the part that was added.

Point at the blue bar in the middle column — those examples cost tokens on every single text you code, which is why few-shot gets expensive at scale. Then the purple in the right column: it appears twice, once in the prompt where you ask for reasoning and once in the reply where you get it. That second appearance is the whole cost story — the model generates all of it before the label arrives, which is where the order of magnitude goes.

Opus scored .995 in all three reply formats on the two hundred sentences, which looked like an artefact until we checked: three different files, three different kinds of reply, and the one sentence all three get “wrong” is this one. Then the other four models: negative in every format they were run in. Fifteen cells, fifteen times negative. Read the sentence. The reviewer is saying the cheese hides bad food. The gold label is wrong; the models are right — and the largest one explains why, in one sentence, better than the gold standard’s author managed with a single click.

Three things follow. First, .995 was never a coincidence across formats: it is the ceiling of this gold standard. Two hundred sentences, one mislabelled, and the best models reach that ceiling in every format. Every “.99” in the Yelp figure is “perfect, minus the mislabelled one”.

Second — and this is the transferable rule — a disagreement between a good model and your gold standard is a question to ask of the gold standard, not only of the model. In your own validation, read every case where your best model and your coders disagree. Some of those are the model’s errors. Some are yours. The gold standard is a measurement too, and it was made by people who were tired at sentence 145.

Third, the arithmetic: a gold standard’s own error rate is a hard ceiling on any accuracy you can measure against it. If your coders were wrong five per cent of the time, no method can score above .95 against them and the ones that do are matching the errors. The paper this deck ends on reports its gold standard’s inter-coder reliability for exactly this reason — a single trained coder agrees with the expert gold at .82 and a majority of three at .88, so no method scored against that gold can be read as more precise than that.

Validation cuts both ways. That is the sentence to leave them with.

Before the first call

  • Personal data. Tweets, comments and interviews are personal data under the GDPR. Sending them to a vendor’s server is a transfer: a processing agreement, a purpose, and usually a data-protection assessment first. Local models avoid the question entirely
  • Terms. Vendors differ on whether your prompts are kept and whether they train on them. Read the terms of the API, not the chat product
  • Disclosure. The EU AI Act puts duties on providers; on researchers the duty is transparency: which model, which version, which prompt, which date, what it cost — the Instrument block of your AI disclosure
  • Ethics review. A method that reads and labels people’s words at scale is still human-subjects research

The first question is not “which model”. It is “may this text leave the building”. Answer it in writing.

What the model brings with it

  • Bias. The model labels the way the text it learned from labels. Törnberg’s GPT-4 leaned “Democrat” as the humans did, and a codebook cannot fully correct a prior it cannot see
  • Contamination. A model that has read the benchmark reproduces its labels: Gilardi et al. lost fourteen points on one task with tweets from after the training cutoff
  • Confident nonsense. The label, the confidence and the rationale are all generated. A fluent reason for a wrong label is the normal case
  • Drift and death. A closed model is updated in place (Chen et al., 2024) and retired eventually, so any result on it is provisional (Ollion et al., 2024). Open weights stay the same file

None of this is an argument against the instrument. It is the list of things the validation has to be designed to catch.

The data are the Playground tool Same text, different name, here for one model, Llama 3.1 8B, the model of the cold open, at temperature 0.8. Hiring: four applicants with identical work histories; the name (and the origin it signals), the stated nationality, age, gender and university were drawn independently for each of them, 1,070 rounds. Coding: forty real posts, each coded under sixty randomly drawn authors.

Read the top panel against the dashed line. If the CV were all that counted, every group would be ranked first 25 times in 100, give or take three by chance. A woman is ranked first almost twice as often as a man, a 30-year-old more than twice as often as a 55-year-old, and a degree from North America or Western Europe more than twice as often as one from South Asia. If the top-ranked applicant were the one invited, men here would be selected at about half the rate of women; US employment guidelines treat anything below four fifths of the most-selected group’s rate as evidence of adverse impact (the “four-fifths rule”).

Then the bottom panel, the same model and the same kind of names on a coding task: the dots sit on the line. Nationality and name origin, not drawn here, move the share coded negative by about a point at most; for moderation (“should a moderator review this post?”) by up to three.

The comparison is the point. With identical CVs the name is the only thing that differs, so the model’s preferences decide the outcome. In coding the post decides, and the name nudges. Real CVs differ, but close calls are exactly where the name would decide. The other two models in the tool lean the same way on gender and university and differ on age; Qwen 3.8 refused most ranking rounds, which the tool shows. All of this holds for these models and these designs; for any other model or task, it has to be measured.
