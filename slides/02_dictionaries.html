Do not explain precision or κ here. The point is only: a number came out, and you have no idea yet whether to believe it. Say that the second half of the lecture is about how you find out.

Last week you learned to count

  • A text became a row of the document–term matrix: one column per word, a count in every cell, and importantly: the order of the words gone
  • That is a representation, but not yet a classification: counting itself does not provide a label (e.g., negative or migration)
  • A measurement or classification adds two things a count lacks: a claim about meaning, and a check that the claim holds
  • This week supplies the “oldest way” of making such a claim, and the general approach of checking it (validation)

Counting → measuring is the step from step 02 of the pipeline to steps 03 and 04.

How tf-idf weights a word

  • Raw counts reward words every speech uses, like government and congress
  • So counts alone cannot show which words set one president apart
  • tf is the share of a president’s words that a word makes up
  • idf is ln(4 ÷ presidents who use the word): 0 if all four use it, 1.39 if only one does
  • tf-idf is tf × idf: high only for a word that is frequent here and rare elsewhere

america is Obama’s most frequent word and scores zero, because all four presidents say it.

Work along one row. america: 204 mentions, about 1 percent of Obama’s words, but all four presidents use it, so idf is ln(4/4) = 0 and the product is zero. isil: nine mentions, 0.04 percent of his words, but only Obama uses it, so idf is ln(4/1) = 1.39 and it outranks america. The unit of comparison is the president (all of a president’s speeches pooled), which is why the fraction has a 4 in it. Next slide: the one call that computes this.

What a count can tell you, and what it cannot

A count can say

  • Which words a corpus uses, and how unevenly
  • Which words set one document apart from the rest
  • How the vocabulary shifts over time

A count cannot say

  • Whether a speech is hopeful or fearful
  • Whether a tweet is about the vaccine, or against it
  • Anything that needs the researcher’s concept rather than the corpus’s words

To get from words to a concept, somebody has to decide which words the concept consists of. That is a dictionary!

Bridge. terror appearing 400 times after 2001 is a fact about attention. Whether the speeches became more fearful is a claim about meaning, and a count does not make claims. Part II is about the oldest way of making one.

II · Dictionaries

The first automated content analysis

The same timeline as last week, opened at its third screen (1958 to 1986). The node to dwell on is 1966, now its own event: the General Inquirer. Stone, Dunphy, Smith and Ogilvie at Harvard put word lists on a mainframe and counted psychological and sociological categories across texts. That is the method of this lecture, sixty years old, and the same year ELIZA showed that pattern matching can fool a reader. One click forward, on the 1990s screen, LIWC (2001) is labelled as the point where this 1966 idea went mainstream in psychology, the version most of the room has met in a paper. Nothing about the method changed in between except the size of the lists and the speed of the machine.

What a dictionary approach is

  • A dictionary is a list of words per category: positive = {good, safe, hope, …}; negative = {bad, threat, war, …}
  • A counting rule: how many words from each list occur in a text, usually per thousand words
  • A decision rule: a text is positive if positive words outnumber negative ones, about migration if any migration word occurs
  • The book is written before the machine sees a text, by the researcher, so the method is deductive

“If word X occurs, the text means Y”: step 03 of the pipeline, and the simplest classifier this course will use.

Four clicks, one per bullet. The book opens with two category pages and empty lines; click 1 fills the word lists; click 2 brings in a text and lights the one word the book knows, with the count; click 3 turns the count into a label. The text is B from last week’s three sentences — “not good news” — so the book’s verdict is wrong, and the class has already seen why. A dictionary is arithmetic on the matrix

# the dictionary: a weight for every column it knows, 0 otherwise
weights <- c(good = 1, news = 1, disaster = -1)

dtm |>
  mutate(score = good * 1 + news * 1 + disaster * -1) |>
  select(doc, good, news, disaster, score)
  • A word list is a technically just a vector of weights over the matrix’s columns: +1, −1, or 0 for the words it does not know
  • The score of a text is its row multiplied by that vector: a weighted row sum
  • Text B scores +2, like text A. not has a column and a zero weight; give it a negative one and “not a disaster” is punished just as hard.

Same matrix as last week. The list only decides which columns count, and with what sign.

What a dictionary score leaves open

  • Whether died in “three people died in the crash” is a negative opinion or a negative fact
  • Whether the texts with no word on the list are neutral, or just use other words
  • Whether “great, another delay” is positive, as the list says, or sarcastic, as you read it
  • Whether a list built from product reviews in 2004 or rated in 2011 is about the same thing as your construct

A dictionary returns a number for every text. Whether the number measures anything cannot be read off the number.

Hu, M., & Liu, B. (2004). Mining and summarizing customer reviews. Proceedings of the 10th ACM SIGKDD International Conference on Knowledge Discovery and Data Mining, 168–177. https://doi.org/10.1145/1014052.1014073

These are general limits of dictionaries, not of one data set. The emotion lines on the last two slides have all four: the lexicon was rated in 2011, many speeches have words it does not know, and anger in a war speech can be a fact about the enemy. Every bullet is a construct-validity question, and every one is answerable only by comparing the list’s verdicts with a human’s. III · Validation

Where a gold standard comes from

  1. Draw a sample of texts the instrument will be judged on — random, or stratified so rare cases are in it
  2. Write a codebook: the categories, and a rule for every case the coders will argue about
  3. Two or more people code the sample independently, without seeing the instrument’s output
  4. Measure their agreement; where it is poor, the codebook is the problem, not the coders
  5. Only then compare the instrument against the human codes

Gold is a metaphor. What you have is the best reading humans can agree on, under rules you wrote.

Krippendorff, K. (2019). Content analysis: An introduction to its methodology (4th ed.). Sage. Chapters 11–12.

Point back to the week 1 slide Content analysis, by hand: this is steps 1, 2, 3 and 5 of that list. Thursday’s lab is exactly this procedure on the review corpus, and the sample they code becomes the reference set for weeks 3 and 4 — say that now so the lab does not feel like an exercise.

Two coders, twenty reviews

PLAYGROUNDTwo coders, one codebook

The twenty reviews and both coders come from the Playground’s Two coders, one codebook: same texts, same codebook, so a student who did the tool on Sunday recognises them.

Five screens. Screen 1: both rows of labels. Screen 2: the thirteen agreements tick, 65%. Ask: is 65% good?

Screen 3 is the one to slow down on. Chance means: coder B keeps the same labels, but in a random order. Some reviews still match, here 7; averaged over many shuffles it is 6.55 of 20, 33%. The sum under it is the same number by hand: B used + eleven times, so of A’s seven + reviews B would give + to 7 × 11 / 20, and likewise for − and ±. Coder A used mixed seven times, coder B never did, so that term is zero.

Screen 4: κ on a ruler. Chance already gets you to 6.55, perfect is 20, so there are 13.45 agreements left to earn. The coders earned 6.45 of them: 6.45 / 13.45 = .48. In shares that is (.65 − .33) / (1 − .33), the formula on the next slide.

Screen 5: a hundred items, 90 negative for both coders, each coder labels ten items positive and they share one. They agree on 82 items, and shuffled labels agree on 82 as well, so the gain over chance is 0 and κ is 0.00. Higher raw agreement than the reviews (82% against 65%) and nothing measured.

The disagreements all have one shape: praise and criticism in one sentence, and a codebook that never said what to do about it. That is what the Two coders tool has them fix.

Cohen’s κ

  • Agreement between two coders, corrected for the agreement they would reach by luck
  • po: the share of items both coders label the same, here 13 of 20 = .65
  • pe: the agreement luck gives when each coder keeps their own label shares, here .33
  • 1 is perfect agreement, 0 is no better than luck

Formula

κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe) = (.65 − .33) / (1 − .33) = .48

coded <- tibble(item = 1:20, coder_a, coder_b)

library(yardstick)        # part of tidymodels
coded |> kap(coder_a, coder_b)

Output

# A tibble: 1 × 3
  .metric .estimator .estimate
  <chr>   <chr>          <dbl>
1 kap     multiclass     0.480

The twenty reviews of the explainer: 65% agreement is a κ of .48.

Three steps, in the order of the explainer. po is the plain percent agreement, the thirteen ticks. pe is what the shuffle gave: for each label, coder A’s share times coder B’s share, added up over the labels (.35 × .55 + .30 × .45 + .35 × 0 = .33). κ is how far the coders got from .33 towards 1, the ruler.

In practice nobody computes this by hand: kap() from yardstick (installed with tidymodels) takes the two columns. It treats the first as “truth”, but κ is symmetric, so the order does not matter. Thursday’s lab computes pe and κ by hand once, so the formula is not a black box.

Cohen, J. (1960). A coefficient of agreement for nominal scales. Educational and Psychological Measurement, 20(1), 37–46. https://doi.org/10.1177/001316446002000104

Krippendorff’s α

  • Disagreement between coders, compared with the disagreement expected by luck
  • Do: the share of items the coders disagree on, here 7 of 20 = .35
  • De: how often two codes drawn at random from all codes pooled differ, here .64
  • Works for any number of coders, with missing codes, and for ordinal or interval scales

Formula

α = 1 − Do / De = 1 − .35 / .64 = .46

codes <- c(coded$coder_a, coded$coder_b)
n_c <- table(codes)                 # 18, 15, 7
N   <- length(codes)                # 40

D_o <- mean(coded$coder_a != coded$coder_b)
D_e <- 1 - sum(n_c * (n_c - 1)) / (N * (N - 1))
round(1 - D_o / D_e, 2)

Output

[1] 0.46

.46 against κ’s .48. They differ because only one coder used mixed.

α counts disagreement instead of agreement. Do is the seven crosses of the explainer. De has a simple reading: put all forty codes of both coders on one pile, draw two at random, and ask how often their labels differ. Of the 1,560 ordered pairs in the pile, 558 carry the same label, so two random draws differ 64 per cent of the time. The coders disagreed 35 per cent of the time, so α = 1 − .35 / .64.

The code is the two-coder, no-missing-data case. For more coders or missing codes the general version builds a coincidence matrix; the R package irr does that with kripp.alpha().

Why α and κ differ: κ builds chance from each coder’s own habits, α from the pooled pile. A’s seven mixed codes inflate A’s share for κ and are diluted in the pile for α.

Krippendorff, K. (2011). Computing Krippendorff’s alpha-reliability. University of Pennsylvania. https://repository.upenn.edu/asc_papers/43

Landis, J. R., & Koch, G. G. (1977). The measurement of observer agreement for categorical data. Biometrics, 33(1), 159–174. https://doi.org/10.2307/2529310

Agreement is not correctness

  • Two coders can agree perfectly and both be wrong: agreement measures shared reading, not truth
  • The codebook decides what the gold standard is: a different rule for “praise and criticism” produces a different gold set from the same texts
  • Every instrument validated against that set inherits the rule, silently
  • So the codebook is part of the measurement, and it goes in the methods section

A gold standard is something people made. Reliability tells you they made it consistently; nothing tells you they made it right.

This is the sentence the Two coders tool ends on. Adding a decision rule sends κ to 1.00 and changes nothing about whether the coding is correct. Thursday’s lab asks for exactly this: the codebook is written, and the rule for praise and criticism decided, before anyone codes a tweet.

Which number do you report?

  • A moderation filter for hate speech wants recall: a missed case does harm; a wrongly flagged one costs a human review
  • A sample of “tweets about the vaccine” for a study wants precision: every false positive pollutes the sample
  • F1 when the two errors cost about the same and you need one number to compare instruments
  • Accuracy only with the base rate beside it; the next slides show why
  • All of them per class: a positive-class F1 says nothing about the negative class

The right metric follows from what an error costs in your study. Decide that in the specification, not after seeing the results.

No call is still an outcome. Before you report a number, decide what happens to these 278 sentences.

Define the term before the numbers. A sentence gets no call in two ways: the list knows none of its words (218 sentences, “Would not go back.”), or it knows some, and they cancel out (60 sentences, like plus blow). The human labels show that silence is not neutrality: two thirds of these sentences are negative. Many are negative complaints phrased without any evaluative word, “Would not go back”, “They never brought a salad we asked for”.

Three ways to count the no-call sentences

Rule Sentences Precision Recall F1
Leave them out 722 0.80 0.97 0.87
Count as negative 1,000 0.80 0.79 0.80
Count as positive 1,000 0.63 0.97 0.76

Coverage, the share of texts an instrument says anything about, is a validity number too. Report it.

  • Leave them out: the list is judged only where it found words, which flatters it
  • Count as negative: the 89 positive reviews among them become misses, so recall falls
  • Count as positive: the 189 negative ones become false alarms, so precision falls
  • Negative is the usual default, because the question is whether the list finds the positive reviews, and saying nothing does not find them. It remains a decision, and the paper has to state it

Precision and recall here are for the positive class. Each rule moves the same 278 sentences into a different cell of the confusion matrix: out of the matrix, into the “said negative” column, or into the “said positive” column. Leaving them out gives the best numbers and describes only 722 of 1,000 sentences. Counting them as negative is the convention when the instrument is meant to detect positive texts: no evidence for positive counts as not positive, and the cost shows up in recall. Counting them as positive is just as possible and moves the cost to precision. Thursday’s lab adds a fourth option for the tweets: call them neutral. Whatever the rule, the methods section has to name it and report the coverage, 722 of 1,000.

Class imbalance

  • The same dictionary, used on corpora where positive texts are more or less common
  • Its accuracy stays at 80% everywhere, so it looks equally good in all of them
  • When positive texts are rare, most of what it flags is wrong: at 5% positives, only 17% of the flagged texts are positive
  • A useless rule, “always say negative”, would even beat it on accuracy

When a class is rare, accuracy says almost nothing. Look at precision and recall.

Nothing about the instrument changes along the x-axis: it always catches 79 per cent of the positive texts and correctly rejects 80 per cent of the negative ones (the dictionary’s numbers from a few slides back). What changes is how many positive texts there are to catch.

Walk the vertical line at 5 per cent. In a corpus of 1,000 texts, 50 are positive and the list flags 40 of them. Of the 950 negative texts it wrongly flags 20 per cent, 190. So it flags 230 texts, and only 40 of those are right: precision 17 per cent. Accuracy still reads 80 per cent, because most of the 950 negative texts are correctly left alone. A lazy instrument that calls every text negative reaches 95 per cent accuracy and finds nothing.

This is the situation of most real measurement tasks in communication research: hate speech, misinformation, a rare frame. The thing you look for is rare.

How sure are you?

Bars: 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, from 1,000 resamples of each size drawn from the 1,000 coded sentences. Dots: median F1.

  • The same dictionary, checked against hand-coded samples of different sizes
  • With 50 coded texts, its F1 could be anywhere between .65 and .91: from unusable to good
  • With 1,000 coded texts, it is pinned between .77 and .82
  • The smaller the validation sample, the less a validation score tells you

A validation set of 50 is a rumour about your instrument. Code more, or report the interval.

The dictionary and the corpus stay the same; only the number of hand-coded texts changes. To see what a small validation sample would have told us, we draw many random samples from the 1,000 coded sentences (with replacement, a bootstrap) and compute F1 in each. The spread of those values is the uncertainty you live with when you code only that many texts.

With 50 texts the same instrument could look unusable or good, depending on which 50 you happened to code. With 1,000 the estimate is pinned down. This is the practical answer to “how many texts should we hand-code”: enough that the interval is narrow for the decision you have to make.

When a word list is the right tool

For

  • Transparent: every rule can be read, and shared
  • Cheap: no training data, runs on a laptop in seconds
  • Deterministic: the same text gets the same label tomorrow
  • Excellent for closed categories — names, actors, topics with a fixed vocabulary

Against

  • Low validity for anything that lives in context: sentiment, frames, irony
  • Silent on texts that use other words; blind to negation
  • A list built for one corpus and decade measures something else on another
  • Encodes its makers’ vocabulary, and their biases, as the construct

Each point on the right calls for a validation result, and no point on the left excuses you from producing one.

Building your own

  1. Define the category in words a coder could apply: the codebook first
  2. Read keyword-in-context listings for candidate words; keep the ones that mean what you think they mean
  3. Add spellings, inflections and lemmas; decide what to do with negation
  4. Score a hand-coded sample; read the false positives and drop the words that cause them
  5. Read the false negatives and find the words you missed
  6. Repeat on a fresh sample — a list tuned on the texts it is scored against will not hold

The Playground’s build-a-dictionary is steps 4 to 6 on twenty texts, with precision and recall updating as you type.

Conclusion: what a dictionary is

  • A dictionary stores what people know about words: which words signal a category
  • Applying it means looking words up, the way you look up an entry in a reference book
  • So it is only as good as that knowledge: who wrote the list, for which texts, and when
  • That is why it has to be validated on your own texts, against human judgement

A dictionary hands human knowledge about words to the computer. Validation checks whether that knowledge fits your texts.
