The world went digital in about twenty years

Hilbert, M., & López, P. (2011). The world’s technological capacity to store, communicate, and compute information. Science, 332(6025), 60–65. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1200970

International Data Corporation. (2024). Global DataSphere and StorageSphere forecasts. IDC. — post-2007 storage.

Epoch AI. (2026). Global AI computing capacity is doubling every 7 months. https://epoch.ai/data-insights/ai-chip-production — AI panel.

The labels need glossing out loud, since none of them are self-explanatory:

Optimally compressed exabytes. They did not count devices or files; they converted everything to the number of bits it would take to store the same content under ideal compression. That is the only way a vinyl record, a VHS tape and a hard disk end up on one axis. One exabyte = a billion gigabytes.

Analog vs digital. Analog is paper, film, vinyl, cassette, VHS. Note the shape: analog capacity does not just lose share, it peaks around 2000 and then shrinks in absolute terms. The blue overtakes it around 2002. The paper itself says only “the early 2000s”; 2002 is what interpolating 25% in 2000 to 94% in 2007 gives, hence the ≈ on the marker. Recent enough that most of the room was alive for it.

General-purpose computing. Machines that can be programmed to do anything: PCs, servers, phones, calculators. Hilbert & López separate these from application-specific computing, the fixed-function chips in a washing machine or a DVD player, which they measured separately and which was much larger in aggregate. Measured in instructions per second, so the axis is log: each gridline is a hundredfold.

Why the compute line is straight. On a log axis a straight line is exponential growth, with a constant doubling time; it looks tame only because the axis is log. If a student asks whether growth slowed: single-thread CPU performance did collapse after Dennard scaling ended around 2005, but aggregate world capacity is core counts times device counts times datacentres, and that kept compounding. AI-specific compute went the other way entirely, doubling every 6–9 months since 2020, far steeper than this line.

The AI line. This one comes from Epoch AI (2026), not Hilbert & López: total AI chip capacity growing ~3.3× a year since 2022, a doubling every 7 months, about 5.2 million operational H100-equivalents by end of 2025. Note that the two right-hand series are in different units: general-purpose is instructions per second, AI accelerators are 8-bit floating-point operations. They share an axis so you can compare shape and order of magnitude, but you cannot read a like-for-like ratio off it. Say that if anyone asks.

The rates. 58% a year for general-purpose computing 1986–2007 is theirs. Carrying that same rate past 2007 is an assumption, and you should say so. Same for the storage figure after 2007: ~12 zettabytes installed by 2025 is an IDC industry estimate. Nobody has repeated their exact inventory since.

Ask before clicking: “how much bigger do you think it gets?” Then click. The 2007 total becomes a sliver you can barely see, which is what the second half of the animation is for.

Communication that was never explicitly elicited for research, but is stored and available at scale. How can we use it?

Parry, D. A., Davidson, B. I., Sewall, C. J. R., Fisher, J. T., Mieczkowski, H., & Quintana, D. S. (2021). A systematic review and meta-analysis of discrepancies between logged and self-reported digital media use. Nature Human Behaviour, 5(11), 1535–1547. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-021-01117-5

Scharkow, M. (2016). The accuracy of self-reported internet use — A validation study using client log data. Communication Methods and Measures, 10(1), 13–27. https://doi.org/10.1080/19312458.2015.1118446

The left column. Ask how they have measured media use so far; almost every answer is one of these three. That is the discipline’s default, and for good reason: an attitude or a motive is not lying around to be picked up, so you have to ask.

The bias claim. Parry et al. (2021) meta-analysed 106 comparisons of logged versus self-reported digital media use: the association is only about r = .38, and self-reports are mostly over-estimates. The error also correlates with what we study: heavy users misreport differently from light users. Scharkow (2016) found the same with client log data. So the bias has a direction, and averaging will not remove it. If someone objects that self-reports are still fine for attitudes, agree; that is the boundary.

The right column. These are old phenomena that now leave a record. Debates were always spoken, but are now transcribed; complaints always existed, but are now reviews with a timestamp.

Do not oversell it. Someone will point out this record is skewed: not everyone posts, platforms shape what is said, APIs decide what you can see. Agree, and say we return to it in the lab: digital traces have their own validity problem, different from that of self-report and no smaller.

Bridge. The obvious objection: it is text, and you cannot run a regression on a tweet. That is the next slide.

Ask what they would do instead. Two answers come up, and both deserve taking seriously:

“Sample it.” A real answer, and it costs you the thing you wanted: the whole decade, and the rare cases. A sample of 500 is perfectly defensible for average tone and useless for “how often were tenants quoted”, because the interesting cases are rare.

“Get more coders.” Also real, also expensive, and it buys a new problem: two coders do not agree with each other. That problem has a name and a number attached, and they meet it in week 2.

Bridge: so either you give up the decade, or you find something that can read 100,000 articles.

The two big questions this course will answer

  1. How can we analyse all of this text — automatically?
  2. How can we make sure that the analysis is valid?

Say both out loud and say that the second one is the harder of the two. Week 1 is about the first; week 2 turns to the second, and the course stays with it from then on.

Objectives and learning goals

After completion of the course, you will…

  1. identify the data-analytic problem in a communication question, state it precisely enough for a machine, and judge whether a computational method can answer it
  2. describe the text-classification pipeline end to end and name where each decision can go wrong
  3. explain what each method costs and assumes, from word counts to large language models, and when the cheap one is right
  4. treat measurement as a research question and decide when a number is not good enough to publish
  5. run a small text-analysis study in a group, from research question to presentation

Only the third names methods. The others outlast whatever tool is current when you graduate.

Read the goals as the exam blueprint, because that is what they are: the exam asks you to frame a problem, to locate a decision in the pipeline, and to judge whether a measurement can be believed. It does not ask you to write code from scratch.

Skills and methods

With regard to the specific methods taught in R, you will be able to…

  • apply dictionaries, supervised classifiers, word embeddings and LLMs to the same texts
  • build a gold standard, compute coder agreement (Cohen’s κ, Krippendorff’s α), read a confusion matrix, and report precision, recall and F1
  • obtain data for a project of your own from APIs, archives and existing corpora

I · Computational communication science

An example: a surprising source of information

  • 2009, Rwanda: a survey of 1,000 customers of the largest mobile provider (demographics, assets, wealth)
  • The researchers also had the call records of 1.5 million subscribers
  • A model trained on the 1,000 predicts wealth from calling behaviour for everyone else
  • Location comes free: the towers that carried the calls

Predicted wealth by district, and by cell tower in the inset. Blumenstock, Cadamuro & On (2015), Science.

Blumenstock, J., Cadamuro, G., & On, R. (2015). Predicting poverty and wealth from mobile phone metadata. Science, 350(6264), 1073–1076. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aac4420

Stop after the second bullet and ask: so far, is this traditional social science? Yes: a survey with a random sample. The computational part is the third bullet: the survey becomes training data. Most of this course has that shape: a small, carefully measured set, scaled up by a model.

Computational social science

  • Social science that uses algorithmic tools and large, unstructured data to study human and social behaviour
  • Complements surveys, experiments and interviews rather than replacing them; the question comes first
  • The usual ingredients
    • Digital traces: posts, logs, transactions, call records
    • Automated text, image, or video analysis
    • Machine learning for classification and prediction
    • Simulation of social processes

Different data, the same questions.

Lazer, D., Pentland, A., Adamic, L., Aral, S., Barabási, A.-L., Brewer, D., … Van Alstyne, M. (2009). Computational social science. Science, 323(5915), 721–723. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1167742

Why now?

  • Big data: social media, digital archives, digitised newspapers, logs of almost everything
  • Constant records: traces of people and organisations accumulate as a by-product of ordinary life
  • More compute: powerful, cheap, and available without owning a computer
  • Sophisticated tools: text classification, topic models, word embeddings, and now large language models, much of it open source

Two to dwell on because they come back in every project: incomplete (a browser log tells you every URL and nothing about why) and algorithmically confounded (what people see on a feed is what a ranking system chose to show them, so the behaviour you observe is partly the platform’s).

What computational methods buy, and what they cost

Opportunities

  • Actual behaviour instead of reports of it
  • People in their own context, not a lab
  • Very large N, and therefore statistical power
  • Patterns that were not observable before

Pitfalls

  • The techniques are demanding
  • The data are often proprietary and cannot be shared
  • Samples are biased in ways that are hard to see
  • Little metadata; unknown provenance
  • Models we no longer understand

Computational communication science

“The emerging subfield that investigates the use of computational algorithms to gather and analyze big and often semi- or unstructured data sets to develop and test communication science theories.”

van Atteveldt & Peng (2018)

  • Large and complex data sets
  • Consisting of digital traces and other naturally occurring data
  • Requiring algorithmic solutions to analyse
  • Used to develop and test communication theory

Van Atteveldt, W., & Peng, T.-Q. (2018). When communication meets computation: Opportunities, challenges, and pitfalls in computational communication science. Communication Methods and Measures, 12(2–3), 81–92. https://doi.org/10.1080/19312458.2018.1458084

Where it shows up

  • Political communication: polarisation, hate speech, campaign messaging, parliamentary debate
  • Journalism: coverage across decades, framing, gender representation
  • Social media use: logged rather than reported use; the spread of information and emotion
  • Health communication: what health information circulates, and where

Three studies, three kinds of data: news text, television, and (for the ethics part) a social network.

Example 1 · Seventy years of nuclear news

  • 51,528 New York Times stories on nuclear technology, 1945–2014
  • Far too many for human coding
  • A topic model finds recurring clusters of words and tracks them over time
  • Research → Cold War → accidents → proliferation

Attention to four topics over time. Jacobi, van Atteveldt & Welbers (2016).

Jacobi, C., van Atteveldt, W., & Welbers, K. (2016). Quantitative analysis of large amounts of journalistic texts using topic modelling. Digital Journalism, 4(1), 89–106. https://doi.org/10.1080/21670811.2015.1093271

Example 2 · Who is on German television?

  • Six channels, 72 sample days, every frame between 08:00 and 24:00
  • A face detector finds and crops every face; a second network estimates gender and age
  • 16 million faces: nobody can watch this much television, but a validated classifier can
  • The classifier is checked against hand-coded faces first; without that step the numbers mean nothing

Detect, crop, classify, validate: the four steps for text, with pixels as input.

Jürgens, P., Meltzer, C. E., & Scharkow, M. (2022). Age and gender representation on German TV: A longitudinal computational analysis. Computational Communication Research, 4(1), 173–207.

This sits next to the news study to show that the pipeline also works beyond text. Detect and crop is obtain, the cropped face is represent, the age/gender network is classify, and the hand-coded check is validate. Say that now: the pipeline slide only comes in part III, and this is the evidence that it generalises.

Example 2 · And the answer is the same everywhere

  • 6.3 million of the 16 million faces were female, a proportion of .39 (95% CI .37 to .42)
  • The imbalance holds across news, sport and advertising, hour by hour and day by day
  • It is also stable over time, so the pattern is structural
  • Women on screen are also, on average, younger than men

Each tile is one hour of one day. Left: share of female faces. Right: estimated age.

Jürgens, P., Meltzer, C. E., & Scharkow, M. (2022). Age and gender representation on German TV: A longitudinal computational analysis. Computational Communication Research, 4(1), 173–207.

Let them look at the left panel for a moment before saying anything. The green wash is the finding, and it is more convincing than the point estimate.

Two things worth drawing out. First, the CI: .37 to .42 is narrow because n is 16 million, and the width of that interval is a measurement question, not a substantive one. Second, the flatness: if the bias were driven by one genre or one time of day you would see stripes. You do not, and that is what makes it structural.

If someone asks how the classifier knows gender: it does not. It predicts a binary label from pixels, it was trained on labelled photographs, and it is wrong some of the time in ways that are not evenly distributed. That is exactly why the validation step exists, and week 2 is about how you would report it.

What is a text?

  • All three are symbols in a sequence, meaningless on their own
  • Meaning arrives only when someone interprets them, in context
  • Automated text analysis tries to bridge that gap

Krippendorff, K. (2018). Content analysis: An introduction to its methodology (4th ed.). SAGE.

Ask which of the three is text. All of them, and the hieroglyphs make the point hardest, because for 1,400 years nobody could read them. The symbols never changed; what was missing was the interpretation. That is Krippendorff’s definition in one image: content analysis makes “replicable and valid inferences from texts to the contexts of their use”, and the context is doing the work.

Read one row aloud: text A has the once, new once, law once … and no not. Then ask what the matrix would look like for 100,000 articles: rows 100,000, columns every distinct word (say 50,000), and almost every cell zero. That is the sparsity point.

What the matrix throws away

Text law good news not disaster
A · The new law is good news. 1 1 1 0 0
B · The new law is not good news. 1 1 1 1 0
C · A disaster, this law. 1 0 0 0 1
  • Five of the ten columns from the previous slide; texts A and B say opposite things and differ in one cell
  • The matrix keeps which words occur and how often; it drops the order they came in. This is the bag-of-words assumption
  • Cheap and surprisingly effective for many questions; blind to negation, irony, and anything that lives between words
  • Treating not as a negative word does not repair it: each column is counted on its own, so “not a disaster” falls just as far as “not good”

Every method in weeks 2 and 3 works on this matrix and inherits this blindness. Week 3 shows the cheap fix, week 4 the expensive one.

This is the sentence to plant now: “not good” is one column away from “good”. Week 2 opens with a tweet the word list gets wrong for exactly this reason, and week 3 asks what a model would need in order to see two words at once.

Content analysis, by hand

  1. Choose the texts
  2. Define the units and the categories
  3. Write a codebook: rules another coder could follow
  4. Code, which is where the time goes
  5. Check agreement between coders, revise the rules, code again
  6. Analyse frequencies, relationships, change over time

Steps 1, 2, 3 and 5 remain when a machine does step 4, and that is the pipeline.

This is the bridge to Part III. Automated content analysis is the same method, with a machine in step 4 and a much larger step 5.

III · The text classification pipeline

The pipeline

Six clicks. The goal is always the same: put a label on texts that do not have one. The last click is the syllabus: weeks 2, 3 and 4 change only what feeds step 03. This figure returns in every lecture; the site’s navigation is built from it.

01 · Obtain

  • Public data sets: parliamentary records, news corpora, research archives; ideal when they exist
  • Scraping: websites and archives you collect yourself; trivial or very hard depending on the site, and always a legal question
  • Third parties: APIs, commercial archives, data donations; custom formats, rate limits, terms of use that change

Obtaining text is rarely the interesting part and often the slowest. Plan for it.

02 · Represent

  • Create features: break text into units (words, n-grams, sentences)
  • Transform: lowercase, strip punctuation, remove stopwords, stem
  • Select: drop very rare and very frequent terms
  • Build the data: the document–term matrix from Part II for counting and classic learning; dense vectors for neural models

Every item on this list is a decision, and every decision changes the measurement. Modern models leave you fewer, but some remain.

03 · Classify

Rule-based Unsupervised Supervised Pre-trained models
Logic “if word X occurs, the text means Y” “these words cluster; I decide what the cluster means” “texts like these were labelled Y, so this one is probably Y too” “given everything I have read, this text is Y”
Meaning comes from the researcher, in advance the researcher, afterwards human-coded training data the model’s training corpus
Example a sentiment dictionary topic models Naive Bayes, SVM, neural nets GPT, Llama, Claude
In this course week 2 — week 3 week 4

04 · Validate

  • Every classifier is a black box to some degree, dictionaries included, and manual coding too
  • The machine does not understand the text; it predicts a label from features, and it is wrong some of the time
  • So the analysis must be shown to be valid: compare its labels with a known good
  • The reference is usually a gold standard: a subset coded by trained humans, with agreement checked

A number becomes a measurement only once it has been validated.

The pipeline runs on problems, not on questions

This is the hinge of the lecture, and the reason the course is called computational analysis rather than automated analysis. Six clicks.

Everything so far has been machinery: four steps, in order, the same every week. But the pipeline cannot start until somebody has turned a question into something it can consume, and that move takes judgement. It is the skill this course is trying to teach.

Do not rush step 4. The moment where two of the three cards dim is the one students remember: a method does not tell you which texts to gather or what to compare them against. Those are decisions you make and then have to defend in writing, and no amount of accuracy rescues a bad one.

If someone objects that the machine could decide the other two as well, good: it could suggest them, but it cannot justify them, and the homework asks for the justification.

Land on the last caption and go straight into the next slide: getting that judgement onto paper is exactly what a specification is.

A computational problem, stated properly

  1. Input: what exactly goes in? One article, one sentence, one post? Whose?
  2. Output: what exactly comes out? A label, a score, a number per month?
  3. Success criterion: what would count as good enough, decided before you run anything
  4. Failure check: how would you find out that you were wrong?

Most labs include a sketch like this, on paper, before you touch a tool.

Most labs have a “sketch first” step built on these four lines, on paper, before any code. Say so now: the framing comes before the code, every week.

The same question, framed twice

Not yet a problem

  • “Is the housing coverage negative?”
  • Negative to whom, measured how, compared with what?

A problem you can run

  • Input: one article
  • Output: negative / neutral / positive
  • Criterion: agrees with a trained human coder
  • Check: on 100 articles nobody used to build it

  • One measurement task runs through weeks 1 to 4
  • One hand-coded gold standard, built by you in week 2
  • Four methods scored against it: counting, a dictionary, a trained classifier, a language model

So by week 4 you can say which method is better, for this task, on this data, rather than which is newer.

V · Ethics

A controversial study

  • 2012: Facebook alters the news feeds of 689,003 users for one week: fewer positive posts for some, fewer negative for others
  • Emotional words in the users’ own posts shift in the same direction; the effect is real and tiny
  • The users were not asked, informed or debriefed
  • Published in PNAS in 2014; the reaction was immediate

The terms of service made it legal; whether it was right is a separate question.

Kramer, A. D. I., Guillory, J. E., & Hancock, J. T. (2014). Experimental evidence of massive-scale emotional contagion through social networks. PNAS, 111(24), 8788–8790. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1320040111

Ethical challenges

  • Privacy: personal data, data breaches, and the question of what you send to a company’s model
  • Bias: models inherit the biases of their training data, and validation is the only way to see it
  • Transparency: complex models are hard to explain, which makes them hard to hold to account
  • Manipulation: the same tools shape opinion and behaviour, with or without consent
  • Cost: energy for training and running models, and ownership of models and data

Van Atteveldt, W., & Peng, T.-Q. (2018). When communication meets computation. Communication Methods and Measures, 12(2–3), 81–92. https://doi.org/10.1080/19312458.2018.1458084

Four principles

  • Respect for persons: treat people as autonomous; honour their wishes
  • Beneficence: understand and improve the risk–benefit profile of the study
  • Justice: risks and benefits should fall on the same people
  • Respect for law and public interest: privacy, copyright, access rights

The first three come from the Belmont Report (1978), the fourth from the Menlo Report (2012). Salganik applies all four to digital research.

Dittrich, D., & Kenneally, E. (2012). The Menlo Report: Ethical principles guiding information and communication technology research. U.S. Department of Homeland Security.

Salganik, M. J. (2018). Bit by bit: Social research in the digital age. Princeton University Press. Chapter 6.

Principles for computational communication research

Principle In practice
1 · Privacy and data security Consent where you can; know where your data go
2 · Fairness Look for bias in data and models; validate
3 · Transparency Prefer interpretable models; share complex ones
4 · Care with experiments Consent and debriefing, or assess the harm
5 · Environmental impact Test small first; most tasks need no giant model
6 · Cross-cultural sensitivity Models do not cross languages for free
7 · Engagement Involve the communities your research touches

Using AI in this course

  • Instrument: the model produces your measurements; disclosed and validated
  • Assistant: it helps you write code or prose that you check yourself; disclosed
  • Thinking partner: it stress-tests ideas that are already yours; disclosed
  • Surrogate: it does the thinking you were supposed to do; not allowed

The rule follows the category of use, whatever the tool. Anything you send to a hosted model leaves your machine.

Point at the AI use page, which has the four categories as a table and three worked disclosure examples. Week 4 hands them both a local model and an API key; the choice between them is theirs to justify.

Example exam question · open

Name and explain two characteristics of big data that make it difficult to use for research. (4 points: 2 for naming, 2 for explaining)

  • Incomplete: created for another purpose, so the variable you need is usually not there. A browser log has every URL and nothing about why.
  • Algorithmically confounded: behaviour on a platform is shaped by the platform’s own ranking, so what you observe is partly the system’s doing.
