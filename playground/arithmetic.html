Weeks 1 to 3 · The arithmetic

Multiply, then add

A dictionary score, a classifier’s verdict and the similarity between two words are the same sum written three ways: multiply each number by a weight, then add them up. This is the one piece of school maths the course leans on, done by hand, on four integers at a time.

What to do · 10 min

  1. Fill in the four missing counts. Mind the zeros.
  2. Score sentences B and C with the word list. Compare B with A.
  3. Do it again with weights a machine learned instead.
  4. Work out why two reviews saying the same thing score differently, and what fixes it.

What you take away

  • What a dot product is, and that you have been reading them since week 1.
  • Why a longer text looks more similar to everything, and what dividing by length does about it.

There is no score and no timer. Show me works every question out in full and costs nothing, so use it as often as you like.

What to notice

A word that is not there gets a zero, not a blank. This is what lets a text be treated as a vector: every text gets a number in every column, in the same order, so any two texts line up position by position. Sentence C is about something else entirely and it still has a good column.

Sentences A and B score the same, and no word list could fix it. B says the news is not good. The matrix does have a column for not (B has a 1 in it), so the tempting fix is to give that column a negative weight. It would work on B, and it would punish “not a disaster” by exactly the same amount, because every column is multiplied on its own and then added. The matrix kept the word but threw away what it was attached to, and no choice of weights puts that back. Much of the course after week 2 follows from this, which is why it keeps returning to these three sentences.

Step 3 changes only the numbers: the row, the sum and the slot stay the same. In week 2 you wrote the weights; in week 3 a model wrote them from a few thousand coded texts, and that is what separates a dictionary from a classifier. It is also why let the data write the dictionary (week 3) is the tool that sits next to this one.

The dot product grows with length, which is a problem. P and Q say the same thing, but Q is twice as long, so P·Q comes out larger than P·P: a text is apparently more similar to a longer copy of itself than to itself. Dividing by both lengths removes how much was written and keeps what it was about. That is cosine similarity, and it is the measurement behind every neighbour list in where words live (week 3).

Nothing in this tool tells you whether good +1 was the right weight, or whether the construct you care about is in those columns at all. Weeks 2 to 4 take up that question.
