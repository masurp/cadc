Week 3 · Naive Bayes

Let the data write the dictionary

A Naive Bayes classifier is a dictionary whose weights were learned from data instead of declared by you. It uses the same bag of words and the same metrics as last week, and every step is arithmetic you can check by hand.

What to do · 12 min

  1. Read the learned word weights on the left.
  2. Drag the training slider from 400 down to 10 and watch test and train pull apart.
  3. Move the threshold slider from −1 to +1.
  4. Tick only the ones it got wrong and read a few.

What you take away

  • How a classifier learns a dictionary from labelled data.
  • What overfitting looks like as a number, and why a bag of words cannot fix some errors.

What to notice

  • The bars on the left are a word list nobody wrote. Compare them with the eight words you typed into build-a-dictionary. The model counted how often each word appears in positive versus negative training texts and turned the ratio into a weight.
  • Very, throughout, plot and long sit near zero. The model worked out on its own that long carries no sentiment, something a hand-written list cannot express, because a dictionary only says “in” or “out”.
  • Drag the training slider from 400 down to 10. Test accuracy falls from ~89% to near chance while training accuracy stays high. That gap is the most important number on screen: the model memorising the texts it was shown.
  • Every tile shows test / train. Only the test figure may go in a paper.
  • Set smoothing α to 0 and the warning appears: a word seen in only one class makes the other class impossible, so one word decides the whole text. Smoothing pretends every word was seen α extra times in both classes, which stops the model dividing by a count of zero.
  • The threshold slider sets the precision/recall trade-off. At −1 you catch nearly every positive text but a third of your calls are wrong; at +1 every call is right but you miss a quarter. No setting maximises both, and which end you want depends on your research question.
  • Tick only the ones it got wrong and read them. “Never boring for a moment” is scored negative; “Well acted, but pointless” positive. More data will not fix these: word order was thrown away at the tokenising step.
  • In that first example, hover never and moment: no weight at all, because neither appears twice in the training texts. The one word that would rescue the sentence is not in the vocabulary. Every word with a small number above it is one you can add up yourself to reach the total.
  • P(positive) reaches 99.9% on texts it gets wrong. Naive Bayes is famously overconfident: it treats every word as independent evidence and multiplies. Report the verdict, not that probability.

Neither the dictionary nor the classifier tells you whether the label you are predicting was worth predicting. That judgement is still yours.
