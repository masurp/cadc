Part III · Weeks 6 to 8

Your own study

For three weeks you work in a group on one question you chose. Here you apply the methods to a study of your own: you find real data, measure something with it, show that the measurement is worth reporting, and defend the whole study in front of the class.

30% of the final grade, one grade per group: see Assessment. Dates and rooms are on Weeks.

THE THREE WEEKS

6
Kickoff · groups, question, data

Get the data

Monday's lecture puts the four weeks back together as one pipeline and covers which data you can obtain in 2026. Then you form groups, pick a question, and go and get data. The one hard rule of week 6: by Thursday you have the data in a file you can open. A plan to obtain it does not count.

  • Due end of week 6: the data-access plan. One page: source, how you get it, legal basis, what you will have in hand, and your fallback if it falls over.
7
No lecture · supervision on Tuesday and Thursday

Measure, then check the measurement

You build the measurement, and then do the part that separates this from a data-journalism piece: hand-code a sample, compute agreement, and report how well your automated measure reproduces it. A project without a validation section cannot pass, however interesting the finding.

8
The mini conference

Present your study

A conference in miniature: a ten-minute talk per group plus five minutes of questions, and a poster gallery over the break. You present what you found, what you could not find out, and which number you would put in a paper. Both the talk and the poster need a validation section and are in English, and everyone attends the whole session: you are the audience too.

GETTING DATA IN 2026

Getting the data is where many projects fail. The 2015 answer, “use the Twitter API”, no longer exists, and a group that spends two of its three weeks negotiating access has no project.

THE THREE-WEEK TEST

Before you commit to a source, answer three questions. If a source fails any of them, it will not work for a three-week project.

  1. Can you download a sample today, without filing an application?
  2. Is there a working R package or a documented endpoint, or are you writing a scraper from scratch?
  3. If it breaks on Wednesday of week 7, what is your fallback?

Bluesky works today, start here atrrr, no key A smaller, unrepresentative and politically skewed population. Say so in your limitations.

YouTube (metadata, comments) works today, needs a free key tuber The daily quota is real. Budget your calls.

Political and institutional text works today ParlaMint, party manifestos, EU documents, court rulings, press releases None, really. The safest route to a good project.
Existing corpora works today Dataverse, Zenodo, Hugging Face, replication packages Read the codebook first: reusing data means inheriting someone else’s decisions.
Data donation possible, plan it in week 6 participants donate their own GDPR data export Needs ethics approval and real recruitment. Only viable if you commit on day one.

X/Twitter, Reddit do not build on these Research access is gone or paid.
DSA Article 40 do not build on this Real, but vetting takes months, not three weeks.
A site that forbids scraping no Ruled out for legal reasons.

Whatever the source, you still need a gold standard: a sample your group hand-coded itself. No project passes without one.

BEFORE YOU COLLECT ANYTHING

Legal basis

Is it personal data?

A public post is still personal data under the GDPR. Ask what you are collecting, why that is the minimum you need, how long you keep it, and where it lives. Pseudonymise on import, not later.

Ethics

Does it need review?

Anything involving participants (data donation, interviews, surveys) needs ethics approval before you start. Public, aggregate, non-sensitive text usually does not. If you are unsure, ask rather than guess.

Hygiene

What never goes in the repo

API keys, tokens, raw personal data, and anything a participant gave you. Keys go in an environment variable; raw data stays local; the repo holds code and a script that fetches. This is graded.

WHAT A GOOD PROJECT LOOKS LIKE

Keep the question narrow

A well-measured claim about one construct in one corpus is better than a sweeping question answered badly. Three weeks is less time than it sounds, and week 8 is the conference, so you cannot use it as extra time.

Validate the measurement

Every group can get a language model to output labels. Your project should show what you did to find out whether those labels are any good, and what you found when you checked.

Report negative results

“The dictionary and the LLM disagree, and here is where and why” is a better project than a headline number nobody checked. You are not graded on the finding being exciting.

Name the limitations

Say what your study cannot show: how you sampled, what the data covers, which population your platform represents, and which classes your classifier never sees. Limitations you name precisely gain marks; leaving them out loses marks.

HOW IT IS GRADED

Criterion What we look for Weight
Question & framing A communication question that a computational method can actually answer, stated precisely, with the construct defined before the tool is chosen 20%
Data & access A defensible source, an honest account of what it covers and what it misses, sampling decisions made on purpose and documented 20%
Measurement & validation A gold standard you built, agreement reported, the automated measure evaluated against it, and the right metric for the class balance you have 30%
Interpretation Conclusions that match the evidence, limitations named precisely, and a clear statement of which number you would defend 20%
Presentation & poster A clear ten-minute talk, a poster that is legible from two metres, and answers that hold up under questioning 10%

Draft rubric: the criteria are fixed, the weights may still move. One grade per group; individual adjustment only where a group reports a genuine imbalance to their supervisor before week 8.
