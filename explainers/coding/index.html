  Where these numbers come from
  
    
    
  • The reviews and the human coding — data/prompt-lab/gold.json, the same
      hand-coded set the prompt-sensitivity tool uses. Each text carries three factual judgements:
      whether the reviewer recommends it, whether the review contains both substantial praise and
      substantial criticism, and whether the overall verdict is clear.
    • 
    
  • The second coder is not invented. The two codings are derived from those facts
      mechanically — broad = mixed whenever both praise and criticism are present;
      narrow = the verdict whenever it is clear. Neither is privileged, and neither was
      written to disagree with you. They are two readings of an instruction that does not say
      which to take.
    • 
    
  • Cohen's κ — (po − pe) / (1 − pe), where
      po is observed agreement and pe is the agreement you would expect from
      two coders guessing independently with the same marginals
      (Cohen 1960).
      For more than two coders, or ordinal categories, use Krippendorff's α instead — same idea,
      fewer assumptions.
    • 
    
  • The part 3 slider is a what-if, not your data. It assumes two categories and two
      coders whose marginals match the base rate, and asks what κ would be at that agreement. The
      arithmetic is exact; the scenario is hypothetical, and it is drawn that way because this is
      the trap most often walked into in published work.
    • 
    
  • This sample over-represents hard cases — 7 of the 20 items are ones where the two
      readings part company. In a real corpus that fraction is usually far smaller, which makes
      overall agreement look better while leaving the rare-category problem exactly as it is.
    • 
    
  • Why the rare category matters — an overall figure is a weighted average dominated by
      the common classes. Reporting agreement per category, or macro-averaging across them, is what
      exposes the category your instrument cannot actually measure.
