Weeks 1 to 3 · the arithmetic

Multiply, then add

Every method in this course ends in the same sum: multiply each number by a weight, then add them up. A dictionary score is that sum. A classifier is that sum. So is the similarity between two words. Four short steps, all integers — you can do every one of them in your head.

How to read the table

One row per sentence, one column per word, and each cell says how many times that word appears in that sentence. Nothing else survives: not the order the words came in, not which sentence came first, and not which word was attached to which.

The five columns here are a stand-in. A real corpus has one column for every word in it, so the table is usually thousands of columns wide and almost entirely zeros.

A word that is not there gets a 0, not a blank. That is what makes each row a vector: every text gets a number in every column, in the same order, every time — so any two texts can be compared position by position. Sentence C is about a different subject entirely and it still has a good column.

Notice that not does get a column, and B has a 1 in it. What the table has no column for is the order the words came in. Hold on to that.

A word list is a row of weights

The dictionary gives every column a weight: good +1 news +1 disaster −1. Words it does not know get 0 — they are still in the sum, they just contribute nothing.

A and B both score +2. Sentence B says the news is not good, and the sum cannot tell. You have just reproduced the central failure of week 2 in five multiplications.

The tempting fix is to give not a negative weight — try it in your head. It would drag B down, and it would drag “not a disaster” down by exactly the same amount, because every column is multiplied on its own and then added. The matrix kept the word and threw away what it was attached to, and no choice of weights can put that back.

It has a name

Multiply position by position, then add. That is a dot product, and the whole of step 2 is written like this:

a · b  =  a1b1 + a2b2 + a3b3 + …

One row of counts, one row of weights, one number out. It is the only sum in this course you will meet more than twice.

Week 3 changes none of the arithmetic. Same row, same sum, same slot — only the numbers in the weight row are different, and the difference is who wrote them. In week 2 you chose them; here the data did.

The data did learn that not leans negative — weight −2, which pulls B down from +5 to +3. It is still positive, and that same −2 is subtracted from every sentence containing not, whatever it is negating. Learning the weights does not change what the matrix kept.

Length gets in the way

Two reviews, two columns. Q says exactly what P says, at twice the length.

Q68
P · Q P · P

So P is more similar to Q than it is to itself — which is nonsense. The dot product grew because Q is longer, not because it is more alike. Divide it out. The length of a vector is √(a·a), so |P| = √25 = 5 and |Q| = √100 = 10.

50 ÷ (5 × 10)

The same two rows, drawn · each row is an arrow

P and Q lie on one line — same direction, different length. R has the same length as P but leans the other way.

cos = 1.00 — P and Q are the same measurement. Dividing by both lengths throws away how much was written and keeps what it was about.

It still discriminates: R is the same length as P but leans differently, and scores 24 ÷ 25 = 0.96. That is cosine similarity, and it is how every comparison between two words is made in the next tool — a dot product, divided by two lengths.
