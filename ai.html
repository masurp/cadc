AI use · 2026

Using AI in this course

AI tools are everywhere, and we cannot realistically make you work without them. What we can do is hold you accountable for what you hand in. You may use AI for most of the work in this course, as long as you say how you used it, can account for everything you hand in, and can own the claims in it. This page explains the four categories of AI use and how to disclose them; the Assessment page explains how the grades are affected.

TWO LANES

We know from our own experience that the competence to use these tools well depends on knowledge you built without them. You cannot judge whether a classifier’s output is any good if you have never worked out what a confusion matrix means on your own. So the course runs two “lanes” on purpose, and always tells you which one you are in.

No AI

The exam and the check-ins

Supervised and closed: no tools, no model, no notes beyond what is allowed in the room. This is where we find out what you can do yourself (reading output, reading code, judging a design), which is the capability the other lane rests on.

AI permitted, but disclosed

Labs, homework and the project

In some parts of the weekly labs, in the homework and in the project, you may use whatever AI tools help you, as long as you disclose them as described below. Where a tool does the routine work, we ask for the judgement instead: why this decision, why this metric, what you rejected and why, and how you know the output is right. In week 4 you use AI as an instrument anyway, and you may also use it as an assistant or a thinking partner. You may not use it as a surrogate: you may not hand in work that was done solely by AI and that you therefore cannot defend without the output in front of you.

FOUR KINDS OF USE

We sort AI use into acceptable and unacceptable categories by what the tool did to the work, and therefore by what you have to be able to account for.

Category What it means What we ask of you
Instrument The model performs a defined task whose output enters your work: classifying your texts, generating code you run, producing labels you analyse. Method-level disclosure, written like a methods section: model and version, how the prompt was designed, and validation against your own judgement.
Assistant Editing, grammar, translation, debugging, help searching. The intellectual content is yours; the tool touched the surface. Which tool, for what, and what you did with its output: what you kept, what you changed, what you rejected.
Thinking partner The model works on your own notes, drafts and materials, helping you stress-test ideas that are already yours. Say what you discussed with it and what came out of it. You must be able to defend every claim without the output in front of you, and the text must be in your own voice.
Surrogate The model produces the work and you put your name on it. Not allowed. It falls under the VU fraud and plagiarism regulation, and no disclosure statement makes it acceptable.

HOW TO DISCLOSE

THE TEMPLATE

Every homework ends with a section C · AI disclosure. Write one block for each category you used, and say which parts you wrote without AI. For each use, answer four questions:

  1. Which tool, and when (e.g. ChatGPT, 12 November; Claude in RStudio; GitHub Copilot)?
  2. What for: which step or section, and what you asked it?
  3. What you did with it: what you kept, what you changed, and at least one thing you rejected, with the reason.
  4. How you checked it: how you know the code does what it says, or the claim is right.

If you used it substantially, paste your most important prompts in an appendix at the end of your file, or add a share link to the conversation.

The standard behind all of it: you must be able to reconstruct and defend the reasoning without the output in front of you. If you cannot, the category was Surrogate, whatever the disclosure says.

Only as an assistant

Assistant. ChatGPT (14 Nov), for one error message in step 4 (“object ‘word’ not found”). It explained that I had removed the column with select() two lines earlier. I fixed the order of the steps myself.

Not used for: the reflections, written without AI.

Example · medium use

Assistant and thinking partner

Assistant. Claude (20 Nov), to write the pivot_wider() call in step 6. I kept its code, checked the table against the confusion matrix by hand (the four cells add up to my 40 tweets), and rejected its suggestion to drop the neutral tweets, because my codebook defines neutral explicitly.

Thinking partner. After writing my error types in B2, I asked Claude whether it saw others. It suggested sarcasm; I checked tweets 12, 31 and 40 and it fits only 31, which I added as an example. The error types are mine.

Not used for: B1 and B3. Prompts in the appendix.

Example · homework 4

When the model is your method

Instrument. llama3.2:3b through Ollama, run on 19 Nov on my laptop, temperature 0, with a JSON schema. The prompt is my week 2 codebook, verbatim in step 2; all 40 replies are in my file. Validated against my gold standard (κ = .58, against .71 between my partner and me).

Assistant. ChatGPT (20 Nov), to turn my codebook into a prompt. It added an instruction to “be careful with sarcasm”, which I removed: it is not in my codebook, and the comparison with my partner’s coding would no longer be fair.

Not used for: the reflections.

NOT ENOUGH

AI use: ChatGPT was used for help with the code and to improve the text.

Which code? Which text? What did you keep, what did you change, what did you reject, and how did you check it? A disclosure like this counts as missing.
