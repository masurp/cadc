Text wrangling & exploration

Week 1 · Lab B

02 REPRESENT Lab B · Text wrangling & exploration Week 1 · 105 min

On Monday you saw how a text becomes numbers in three moves: split it into tokens, count them, and arrange the counts in a document–term matrix. Today you make those three moves yourself in R, first with the three sentences from the lecture, where you can check every number by eye, and then with more than two centuries of State of the Union speeches. Along the way you make your first plots with ggplot.

The package that does the text work is tidytext, which turns a text into a table with one row per word. From then on, everything you learned on Tuesday (filter(), mutate(), group_by(), summarize()) works on text too.

From the lecture to the lab

  • From text to numbers, From tokens to counts and The document–term matrix: section 1 uses the same three sentences and rebuilds the same matrix.
  • What the matrix throws away: Exercises 1 and 2. Sentences A and B differ in one word and mean the opposite.
  • The pipeline: today is steps 01 (obtain) and 02 (represent). Nothing is classified yet; that starts next week with dictionaries.
  • Content analysis, by hand: counting is where content analysis started, and the questions you ask in Part 2 (what do people write about, and does it differ between groups?) are its classic questions.

The data

Part 1 uses the sotu package: all 240 State of the Union addresses from 1790 to 2020, the yearly report of the US president to Congress. From 1801 to 1912, and occasionally after that, the address was sent as a written message rather than given as a speech; the column sotu_type says which. One row is one address, with the president, the year and the party. Together they hold about two million words.

Part 2 uses 5,002 tweets from the turn of the year 2014/15 that carry the hashtag #NewYearsResolution. Crowd workers sorted each tweet into one of ten categories (Health & Fitness, Personal Growth, Humor, …), and the data records the gender and US region of the person who tweeted. User names are removed and @-mentions replaced by @user. One row is one tweet.

Part 1 · From text to numbers

1 · Three sentences, one matrix

These are the three sentences from the lecture. We store them in a small table with one row per document.

library(tidyverse)
library(tidytext)

texts <- tibble(
  doc  = c("A", "B", "C"),
  text = c("The new law is good news.",
           "The new law is not good news.",
           "A disaster, this law."))
texts

unnest_tokens() splits the text into tokens. The first argument is the name of the new column (word), the second the column that holds the text. It also lowercases everything and removes punctuation.

tokens <- texts |>
  unnest_tokens(word, text)
tokens

Now we count how often every word occurs in every document, and turn the result on its side with pivot_wider(): one row per document, one column per word. This is the document–term matrix from the lecture.

tokens |>
  count(doc, word) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = word, values_from = n, values_fill = 0)

count(doc, word) is a shortcut for group_by(doc, word) |> summarize(n = n()). values_fill = 0 writes a 0 where a word does not occur in a document.

5 · Your first ggplot

A plot in ggplot is built from three parts, added with +: the data, the aesthetics (aes(): which column goes on which axis), and a geom (what to draw: bars, points, lines).

top_words <- clean_words |>
  count(word, sort = TRUE) |>
  head(15)

ggplot(top_words, aes(x = reorder(word, n), y = n)) +
  geom_col(fill = "steelblue") +
  coord_flip() +
  labs(x = "", y = "Number of times used",
       title = "The most frequent words in 240 State of the Union speeches") +
  theme_minimal()

reorder(word, n) sorts the bars by their count; coord_flip() turns the chart so that the words are readable.
