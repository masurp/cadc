Few-shot, structured output & sensitivity

Week 4 · Lab A

03 CLASSIFY Lab A · Few-shot, structured output & sensitivity Week 4 · 105 min

Today a language model does the coding. You write the codebook as a prompt, send it to a model running on your own laptop, and get a label back for every text. Then you do what the lecture called the harder work: find out how much the labels depend on the way you asked, and on the model you asked.

Everything runs locally, through Ollama: no account, no costs, and no text leaves your laptop. The models are small (3 and 8 billion weights), much smaller than the chatbots you know, which makes their weaknesses easier to see and their results reproducible. If your laptop cannot run them, you use the course key for a small model on OpenAI’s servers instead.

The data

Part 1 uses the lecture’s 48 one-sentence film reviews. The course team coded each review on three factual questions: does it recommend the film, does it contain both praise and criticism, and is its verdict clear? The two codebooks, broad and narrow, are derived from those answers mechanically, so neither is the “right” one. They disagree on 13 reviews, the borderline cases such as “Slow, but worth it”. One row is one review with both gold labels.

Part 2 uses the scientific abstracts from week 3, but only 30 of them. A language model needs no training data, so these 30 coded abstracts are all test data: their labels are the gold standard you validate the model against.

Part 1 · Film reviews, coded by a model

1 · The first call

This is the lecture’s first example. llm_message() builds the message, chat() sends it to a model (here: Ollama on your laptop), and get_reply() returns the answer as text. The first call takes a little longer, because the model has to be loaded into memory.

library(tidyverse)
library(tidyllm)
library(yardstick)

llm_message("In two sentences: what is a codebook in content analysis?") |>
  chat(ollama(.model = "llama3.2:3b", .temperature = 0)) |>
  get_reply()

# with the course key, in this call and every call below:
# chat(openai(.model = "gpt-5.4-nano", .temperature = 0))

.temperature = 0 makes the model take the most probable word every time, so the same prompt gives the same answer, which is what you want for measurement.

With the course key, write openai(.model = "gpt-5.4-nano", .temperature = 0) instead of ollama(.model = "llama3.2:3b", .temperature = 0), as the comment in the code says. A model on OpenAI’s servers is not perfectly repeatable even at temperature 0: a rerun can change a label now and then.

4 · A prompt is a codebook

We write the codebook as a text with a placeholder {text}, and replace the placeholder with one review. This is the broad codebook:

codebook <- "You are coding film reviews for a research project.
Label the review as positive, negative, or mixed.
Use 'mixed' whenever the review contains BOTH substantial praise AND substantial
criticism, even if one outweighs the other.

Review: {text}"

prompt <- str_replace(codebook, stringr::fixed("{text}"), reviews$text[30])
cat(prompt)

llm_message(prompt) |>
  chat(ollama(.model = "llama3.2:3b", .temperature = 0)) |>
  get_reply()

stringr::fixed() tells str_replace() to look for the literal characters {text}.

5 · Structured output, and 48 reviews

A free-text reply has to be parsed. A schema forces the model to answer with one of the allowed labels. code_one() wraps the whole call in a function, and map_chr() runs it on every review.

schema <- tidyllm_schema(
  name  = "coding",
  label = field_fct("the code", .levels = c("positive", "negative", "mixed")))

code_one <- function(text, model = "llama3.2:3b") {
  llm_message(str_replace(codebook, stringr::fixed("{text}"), text)) |>
    chat(ollama(.model = model, .temperature = 0, .json_schema = schema)) |>
    get_reply_data() |>
    pluck("label")
}

labels <- c("positive", "negative", "mixed")

coded <- reviews |>
  mutate(pred = map_chr(text, code_one, .progress = TRUE)) |>
  mutate(across(c(broad, narrow, pred), \(x) factor(x, levels = labels)))

coded |> accuracy(truth = narrow, estimate = pred)

across() turns the three label columns into factors with the same three levels, which yardstick needs to compare them.

Part 2 · On your own: do the examples decide the label?

ABOUT 30 MINUTES · AI PERMITTED, DISCLOSE IN ONE LINE

Open week04-lab-a-part2.qmd from the lab folder; science-abstracts.csv from week 3 is next to it.

Back to week 3’s abstracts. In week 3 you split them into training and test data: a classifier learns its rules from coded examples, so it has to be tested on abstracts it did not learn from. A language model needs no training data from you. Others trained it, on other texts, and your coded abstracts change nothing in it. So here you do not split the abstracts, as Monday’s slide The steps, with a language model showed. You draw 30 of them, 10 per discipline, as the test set: their labels are the gold standard you validate the model against. Thirty keeps one run to a minute or two.

In Exercise 2 you added three coded examples to the prompt; here you test what the choice of examples does. The examples are part of the prompt: the model reads them along with every abstract, and its weights do not change. They must still come from outside your 30 test abstracts, or the model would see the right answer for an abstract it is tested on. Two prompts give the model the three categories and three coded example abstracts, and differ only in which examples:

  • Prompt A: one example from each discipline.
  • Prompt B: three examples from statistics.

Your question: Do the examples in a prompt decide which label the model gives?

Take 30 abstracts (10 per discipline) and, as examples, short abstracts that are not among those 30. The skeleton gives you a function that builds a prompt from three examples. Run both prompts with a schema, and report the accuracy of each, the share of abstracts whose label changed, and how often each prompt answered each discipline. Before you run them, write down which prompt you expect to answer statistics more often, and by how much.
