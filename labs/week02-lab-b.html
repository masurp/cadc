Validation lab

Week 2 · Lab B

04 VALIDATE Lab B · Validation lab Week 2 · 105 min

On Tuesday the dictionary gave every tweet a verdict. Nothing you did told you whether those verdicts were right. For that you need what the lecture called a gold standard: texts coded by humans, following a codebook, with a known level of agreement between the coders. Today you build one yourself, in pairs, and then learn to compare a machine’s verdicts with it: the confusion matrix, accuracy, precision, recall and F1.

Every method in weeks 3 and 4 is judged the way you learn today, and the gold standard you build comes back in week 4.

From the lecture to the lab

  • Where a gold standard comes from and Two coders, twenty reviews: sections 1 to 3. Today you are the two coders.
  • Cohen’s κ: section 4 uses the lecture’s code.
  • Agreement is not correctness: sections 5 and 6, where you read your disagreements and turn two codings into one gold standard.
  • The confusion matrix, Accuracy, precision, recall, F1 and The 278 silent sentences: section 7, on the lecture’s own data.
  • Which number do you report?: the trap in section 7, and Part 2.

The data

Parts 1 and 2 use 40 of Tuesday’s #corona tweets: each pair draws its own sample, after removing repeated copies of the same tweet. You and your partner code the same 40.

Section 7 uses the lecture’s Yelp data: 1,000 single sentences from restaurant reviews on Yelp, each labelled positive or negative by the researchers who published the set (Kotzias et al., 2015). Exactly half are positive. One row is one sentence.

Part 1 · A gold standard, by hand

1 · The codebook comes first

ON PAPER · IN PAIRS · 10 MIN

Together with your partner, and before you look at any tweet, write a short codebook:

  1. Three labels, pos, neg and neu, each defined in one sentence.
  2. One rule for a tweet that contains both praise and criticism.
  3. One rule for a tweet that only reports a fact (“500 new cases today”).

Take ten minutes at most. You will have to code by these rules.

2 · Draw your tweets

Use your pair number as the seed, so that you and your partner draw the same 40 tweets, and other pairs draw different ones. We first remove exact duplicates: on Tuesday you saw that some tweets were posted dozens of times.

library(tidyverse)
library(tidytext)

tweets <- read_csv("corona-tweets.csv")

set.seed(1)                                    # change 1 to your pair number
sample_40 <- tweets |>
  distinct(text, .keep_all = TRUE) |>
  slice_sample(n = 40) |>
  select(id, text)

cat(paste0(1:40, ". ", sample_40$text), sep = "\n\n")

cat() prints the tweets as plain, numbered text, which is easier to read than a table.

Fill in your 40 labels in the order the tweets are numbered. Do not talk to your partner and do not look at their screen. Once you have coded a tweet, do not go back and change it.

5 · Read the disagreements

coded |>
  filter(coder_a != coder_b) |>
  select(text, coder_a, coder_b)

Read every disagreement out loud with your partner. For each one, decide: was it a slip, or a case the codebook did not cover? Count the second kind, and change the codebook so that it covers them.

6 · Your gold standard

Agree on one label for every tweet you disagreed on, using your improved codebook. Start from coder A’s labels and overwrite the ones you changed, by their row number:

gold <- coded |>
  mutate(label = coder_a) |>
  select(id, text, coder_a, coder_b, label)    # keep both coders: Homework 2 needs them

gold$label[c(3, 17)] <- c("neg", "neu")        # your resolved rows and labels

gold |> count(label)
write_csv(gold, "gold-corona.csv")

Keep gold-corona.csv safe. You need it for the homework, and it comes back in week 4, when a language model codes the same tweets with your codebook.

7 · A dictionary against a gold standard

Before you test Tuesday’s dictionary on your own tweets, practise on the lecture’s data: 1,000 restaurant review sentences, each labelled positive or negative by a human. This is the lecture’s code: Bing verdicts, computed as on Tuesday.

bing <- get_sentiments("bing")
yelp <- read_csv("yelp-sentences.csv")

scores <- yelp |>
  unnest_tokens(word, text) |>
  inner_join(bing, by = "word") |>
  count(id, sentiment) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = sentiment, values_from = n, values_fill = 0) |>
  mutate(score = positive - negative)

verdicts <- yelp |>
  left_join(scores, by = "id") |>
  mutate(verdict = case_when(score > 0 ~ "positive",
                             score < 0 ~ "negative",
                             TRUE      ~ "no call"))

called <- verdicts |>
  filter(verdict != "no call")

called |> count(label, verdict)                # the confusion matrix
mean(called$label == called$verdict)           # accuracy

Part 2 · Homework 2: is Tuesday’s dictionary right?

START IN THE ROOM · FINISH AT HOME · AI PERMITTED, DISCLOSE IT

Open week02-lab-b-part2.qmd from the lab folder. Part 1 saved your gold-corona.csv in the same folder, with your labels, your partner’s labels and the resolved gold label. Use the rest of the session for the first steps, and finish at home.

Your question: How well does the Bing dictionary measure the sentiment of #corona tweets, judged against your own gold standard?

Your gold standard has three labels; the dictionary has positive, negative and no call. Deciding how these relate is part of the task, and there is more than one defensible answer.

The skeleton takes you through nine steps:

  1. Load your gold standard and the dictionary.
  2. Your gold standard: how often each label, and your κ with your partner.
  3. Score the 40 tweets with Bing, as on Tuesday.
  4. A verdict per tweet, with your decision on what a no call and a tie mean.
  5. The confusion matrix.
  6. Accuracy, and precision and recall per class.
  7. The other decision: recompute the accuracy with the opposite rule for no call.
  8. You or your partner? The dictionary’s agreement with each of you separately.
  9. The errors: every tweet the dictionary got wrong, with the words it matched.

Then three short reflections on your own results, and your AI disclosure.
