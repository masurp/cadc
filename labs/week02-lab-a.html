Dictionary approaches

Week 2 · Lab A

03 CLASSIFY Lab A · Dictionary approaches Week 2 · 105 min

Today you label texts for the first time. The method is the one from Monday’s lecture: a dictionary is a list of words, each with a category attached, and the computer counts how many words of each category occur in a text. Sum the counts and you have a score; turn the score into a verdict and you have a label for every text. You go through the analysis step by step, as the lecture did, and then you open it up to see which words did the work.

From the lecture to the lab

  • What a dictionary approach is and A dictionary is a table: section 3. The dictionary is a plain two-column table, and applying it is a join.
  • The dictionary analysis, step by step 1 to 3: sections 4 and 5 follow those slides line by line (tokens, join, count per text, score).
  • Feature engineering for a word list: Exercise 1, on lemmatising.
  • What the line does not tell you: sections 6 and 7. You build the plot of ten days of #corona yourself, find out what it counts, and read the tweets behind the most influential words.
  • Building your own: Part 2, where you write a word list for a topic yourself.

The data

Part 1 uses 2,855 English-language tweets that carry the hashtag #corona, posted between 5 and 14 November 2021 and collected by the course team for the 2021 edition of this course. Retweets and non-English tweets were removed, and user names replaced by @user. One row is one tweet, with the time it was posted and how often it was liked and retweeted.

The dictionary is the Bing sentiment lexicon (Hu & Liu, 2004): 6,786 English words, each marked positive or negative. It was built from customer reviews of products, and section 7 comes back to that.

Part 2 uses the State of the Union speeches from last week.

4 · Tokens, and the join

We split the tweets into words, and then keep only the words that are in the dictionary. inner_join() does exactly that: it keeps the rows that have a match in both tables, and adds the sentiment column.

tokens <- tweets |>
  unnest_tokens(word, text)

hits <- tokens |>
  inner_join(bing, by = "word")
hits

5 · From words to a verdict per tweet

We count the positive and negative words per tweet, put them side by side, and compute a score: positive minus negative.

scores <- hits |>
  count(id, sentiment) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = sentiment, values_from = n, values_fill = 0) |>
  mutate(score = positive - negative)
scores

Exercise 2: Join scores back to tweets so that every tweet is kept, and add a column verdict that says "positive" if the score is above 0, "negative" if it is below 0, and "no call" otherwise. 6 · Sentiment over ten days

This is the code behind the plot in the lecture.

daily <- hits |>
  mutate(date = as.Date(created_at)) |>
  count(date, sentiment) |>
  group_by(date) |>
  mutate(share = n / sum(n))

daily |>
  filter(sentiment == "negative") |>
  ggplot(aes(x = date, y = share)) +
  geom_line() +
  geom_point() +
  labs(x = "", y = "Share negative") +
  theme_minimal()
