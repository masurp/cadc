Supervised classification

Week 3 · Lab A

03 CLASSIFY Lab A · Supervised classification Week 3 · 105 min

Last week you wrote the word list yourself. Today an algorithm writes it: you give it texts with a known label (the training set), it learns which words go with which label, and you test the resulting classifier on texts it has never seen (the test set). This is supervised classification, and you build the lecture’s workflow step by step with tidymodels: split, recipe, model, workflow, fit, predict, evaluate. Then you swap the algorithm while the recipe and the evaluation stay the same, and compare the results.

From the lecture to the lab

  • The pipeline with a classifier: the sketch in section 2.
  • Hold part back and Testing is validation: section 3.
  • A classifier, end to end (the recipe in four steps, the workflow, specify and fit, predict the test set, the confusion matrix three by three, the numbers you report): sections 4 to 6 use the lecture’s code.
  • Swap the algorithm and Three algorithms, one test set: section 7.
  • Where every method lands, the table from van Atteveldt et al. (2021): Part 2, where you try to beat that table’s machine-learning rows.

The data

Part 1 uses the lecture’s 5,250 abstracts of scientific papers from arXiv, the online archive where researchers share their papers: 1,750 each from computer science, physics and statistics. The label is the discipline the authors filed the paper under. One row is one paper, with its title and abstract.

Part 2 uses the data behind van Atteveldt, van der Velden and Boukes (2021), the paper that closed the lecture: 6,322 headlines from Dutch newspapers and news sites about the economy, from February to July 2015, each hand-coded as negative, neutral or positive. 284 of them are the paper’s test set, on which its Table 2 is computed. You do not need to read Dutch.

1 · Getting to know the data

The first chunk loads tidyverse, tidymodels (split to evaluation) and textrecipes (the text steps for tidymodels), reads the abstracts and counts them per discipline.

library(tidyverse)
library(tidymodels)
library(textrecipes)

science <- read_csv("science-abstracts.csv") |>
  mutate(label = factor(label))

science |> count(label)

science has one row per paper: id, label (the discipline), title and text (title plus abstract). tidymodels needs label as a factor, a variable with a fixed set of categories, to treat the task as classification. With 1,750 abstracts per discipline, always predicting the same one gives an accuracy of .33, the floor any classifier has to beat clearly.

Before any model reads these texts, read one abstract per discipline yourself (run the chunk a few times to see others). Could you tell them apart? Which words gave the discipline away?

Sections 3 to 6 turn your sketch into the seven steps of the lecture’s workflow. The chips under each heading show where you are: green for the steps the section covers, grey for those already done.

Step Function What it does
split initial_split() puts part of the labelled abstracts aside as a test set
recipe recipe(), step_*() describes how a text becomes numbers
model multinom_reg() chooses the algorithm and its settings
workflow workflow() joins the recipe and the model into one object
fit fit() trains the recipe and the model on the training set
predict predict() lets the trained model label the test set
evaluate conf_mat(), metric_set() compares those labels with the true ones

The first step puts 20% of the abstracts aside. The model never sees them during training, so they can stand in for the new texts it will classify later.

set.seed(42)                                   # the split is reproducible
split <- initial_split(science, prop = .8,     # 80% training, 20% test
                       strata = label)         # each class keeps its share

train_data <- training(split)
test_data  <- testing(split)

nrow(train_data)
nrow(test_data)
test_data |> count(label)
  • set.seed(42) fixes the random draw of initial_split(), so you get the lecture’s split.
  • prop = .8 gives 4,200 abstracts for training and 1,050 for the test.
  • strata = label draws within each discipline, so each keeps its share: 350 test abstracts per discipline.
  • training() and testing() take the two sets out of the split.

Leave test_data alone until section 6: a model always looks better on texts it has already seen.

4 · The recipe

splitrecipemodelworkflowfitpredictevaluate

A recipe writes down how a text becomes numbers. It learns what it needs from the training data and applies it unchanged to every later text, the test set included. These are the lecture’s four steps:

rec <- recipe(label ~ text, data = train_data) |>   # predict label from text
  step_tokenize(text) |>
  step_stopwords(text) |>
  step_tokenfilter(text, min_times = 20, max_tokens = 1000) |>
  step_tf(text)
  • recipe(label ~ text, ...) predicts label (left of the ~) from text (right of it).
  • step_tokenize() splits each abstract into words, like unnest_tokens() in week 1.
  • step_stopwords() drops frequent English words such as the and of, which occur in every discipline.
  • step_tokenfilter() keeps the 1,000 most frequent of the words seen at least 20 times in the training abstracts (3,690 words qualify). Rare words teach the model nothing reliable, and fewer columns fit faster.
  • step_tf() counts how often each kept word occurs in each abstract (term frequency).

A recipe does nothing until you use it: prep() learns the 1,000 words from the training data, and bake() applies them (new_data = NULL means the data it was prepared on). The result is week 1’s document–term matrix:

rec |> prep() |> bake(new_data = NULL)

It has 4,200 rows and 1,001 columns, mostly zeros: the label plus one count per word, such as tf_text_quantum. The test abstracts are later counted on the same 1,000 words; a word that occurs only in the test set is ignored. You only call prep() and bake() here to look: the workflow in section 5 runs both for you.

5 · Model, workflow, fit

splitrecipemodelworkflowfitpredictevaluate
logit_spec <- multinom_reg(penalty = 0.01, mixture = 1) |>
  set_engine("glmnet") |>
  set_mode("classification")

logit_wf <- workflow() |>
  add_recipe(rec) |>
  add_model(logit_spec)

m_logit <- fit(logit_wf, data = train_data)
  • multinom_reg() is the lecture’s logistic regression for more than two classes: a weight per word per discipline, summed and turned into three probabilities. penalty = 0.01 punishes large weights, so 1,000 words cannot memorise 4,200 abstracts (overfitting); mixture = 1 (the lasso) sets many weights to exactly zero.
  • set_engine("glmnet") names the R package that does the computing, and set_mode("classification") says the outcome is a category.
  • workflow() joins recipe and model, so every text passes through the same recipe first.
  • fit() does the learning; until then, the specification only describes the model. It prepares the recipe on train_data and estimates the weights in about ten seconds. m_logit is the trained classifier.

6 · Predict and evaluate

splitrecipemodelworkflowfitpredictevaluate

Now the test set comes back. predict() runs the 1,050 test abstracts through recipe and model and returns one column, .pred_class, in the row order of test_data; bind_cols() puts the true label next to it. Last week you counted the confusion matrix and computed precision and recall by hand; here conf_mat() and metric_set() do that. conf_mat() shows the truth in the columns and the predictions in the rows, with the hits on the diagonal. With three classes, precision, recall and F1 are averaged over the disciplines (macro).

7 · Swap the algorithm

splitrecipemodelworkflowfitpredictevaluate

Trying another algorithm now means changing one object, the model. Split and evaluation stay the same, so a difference in the scores comes from the algorithm alone. The lecture’s linear support vector machine (SVM) is sensitive to the scale of its inputs, so its recipe gets one extra step, step_normalize(all_predictors()), which puts every word column on the same scale (mean 0, standard deviation 1). That is why the recipe chip is green too.

svm_wf <- workflow() |>
  add_recipe(rec |> step_normalize(all_predictors())) |>
  add_model(svm_linear() |>
              set_engine("LiblineaR") |>
              set_mode("classification"))

m_svm <- fit(svm_wf, data = train_data)

pred_svm <- predict(m_svm, new_data = test_data) |>
  bind_cols(select(test_data, label))

Naive Bayes, the lecture’s “dictionary the data wrote”, learns from the counts how typical each word is of each discipline. In its specification below, usepoisson = TRUE says the inputs are counts, and Laplace = 1 adds one to every count, so a word never seen in one discipline does not rule it out. It needs raw counts, so it takes the original rec without normalisation.

Part 2 · On your own: beat the paper

ABOUT 35 MINUTES · IN THE ROOM · NOT HANDED IN · AI PERMITTED

Open week03-lab-a-part2.qmd from the lab folder; ecosent-headlines.csv is next to it. This part stays in the room; this week’s homework is the second part of Thursday’s lab.

You know this data from the lecture: 6,322 Dutch newspaper headlines about the economy, hand-coded as negative, neutral or positive by van Atteveldt, van der Velden and Boukes (2021). Besides id and label, the file has headline as printed (Rabobank voorspelt flinke stijging hypotheekrente); lemmata, the same with every word in its dictionary form (Rabobank voorspellen flink stijging hypotheekrente), so different forms of a word count as one; and set: train for the 6,038 headlines you may learn from, test for the 284 on which the paper reports its results:

Model in the paper Accuracy
Naive Bayes .58
Support vector machine .57
Convolutional neural network .63
(Best dictionary, week 2) .46

Your question: Can a classifier you build beat the paper’s machine-learning results on the same test headlines?

The rules

  • Train only on the headlines with set == "train". The 284 test headlines are your test_data.
  • Compare settings on a validation part: split the training headlines once more with initial_split().
  • Use the test headlines once, at the end, for the setting you chose. If you try several settings on them and keep the best, the test set has helped to choose, and its score no longer shows how the model does on unseen headlines.

What you can change

You can change any step of the Part 1 workflow, in any combination. None of these options needs an extra package.

In the recipe:

  • Use headline instead of lemmata, in recipe() and in every step.
  • Drop Dutch stop words: step_stopwords(lemmata, language = "nl") after step_tokenize().
  • Add pairs of neighbouring words (flink stijging): step_tokenize(lemmata, token = "ngrams", options = list(n = 2, n_min = 1)).
  • Keep more or fewer words with max_tokens and min_times in step_tokenfilter(). Without max_tokens, it keeps only 100.
  • Weight counts by how rare a word is, like week 2’s tf-idf: step_tfidf() instead of step_tf().

In the model:

  • Logistic regression with another penalty: smaller uses more words, larger fewer. With too large a penalty, every weight is zero and the model predicts one label for all headlines. Keep max_tokens at 1,000 or below: with 2,000 words, one fit takes minutes instead of seconds.
  • Naive Bayes (Exercise 2) with another Laplace. It needs counts from step_tf().
  • The linear SVM (section 7), with step_normalize(all_predictors()) in the recipe. A larger cost lets it follow the training headlines more closely.

Other algorithms, such as a random forest, are listed at tidymodels.org; most need one more package.

How to work

Treat it as a small experiment:

  1. Start with the workflow from Part 1, adapted to lemmata. Train it on the training part and check its accuracy on the validation part. This is your first result.
  2. Change one thing, for example the number of words or the algorithm, and check again. If you change two things at once, you cannot tell which one made the difference. Write each attempt and its accuracy in the table in the skeleton.
  3. Compare every accuracy with the majority baseline: the accuracy you would get by always guessing the most common label, neutral. That guess is right for about four in ten training headlines, so a model has only learned something from the words if it does clearly better.
  4. When you have found your best version, train it once more on all training headlines (training and validation part together), and test it on the 284 test headlines. Do this once, at the very end, and compare the result with the paper.

The headlines come back in week 4, when a language model takes the same test.
